The Ephod & the Breastplate

The concordance is this app's heart, and colour is already its language. The high priest's ephod carried the breastplate: twelve stones in four rows over his heart, the tribes camped around the tabernacle under their standards, and the garment itself woven of gold, blue, purple, scarlet, and fine linen. Every claim below carries its chapter and verse on its face. What has no chapter and verse is labelled tradition, in the open.

How to read the labels: Scripture — the chapter and verse say it. Tradition — handed down outside Scripture, and labelled so. Design — how this screen draws it: a depiction, never a claim. The same act can stand differently in different places — a census was once required (Exodus 30:11–16; Numbers 1) and later counted a sin (2 Samuel 24; 1 Chronicles 21). Only the references show which is which, so the references travel with every claim here.

Panel A — The Breastplate · Exodus 28:17–21

The order of the stones and their names: Scripture — Exodus 28:17–20; Exodus 39:10–14. The tribe on each stone: tradition — the commonly printed chart (Scripture gives the order and the names engraved, never the pairing stone by stone). The colours on screen: DEPICTION design. Tap a stone for its tribe, its colour, its verses, and its Strong's entry.

Tribe–stone pairing: tradition — commonly printed chart.

The pairing note, carried whole: the chart above is the one most commonly printed (Adam Clarke's arrangement), and the names follow the engraving order of Exodus 39:14 — the second son's name standing on the second stone, and so on. One reading, by strict birth order, pairs six of the middle stones differently: rows one and four stand the same on both readings, and so does Judah's; the two middle rows change. Scripture itself settles neither chart: it gives the order of the stones and commands the names engraved, and never pairs them stone by stone. A further exchange (Gad and Naphtali) in the midrash's flag list is carried in Panel B, where it belongs.

Panel B — The Camp · Numbers 2

Positions: Scripture — Numbers 2. Flag colours and emblems: tradition — Numbers Rabbah. Diagram styling and screen shades: design. Flag colours follow the midrash's own pairing and may differ from the stone colours in Panel A. Tribes are listed on each side in the order the chapter names them.

Panel C — The Woven Colours · Exodus 28

Colour names and materials: Scripture — Exodus 28. Screen shades and gradients: design. The ephod was woven of these (Exodus 28:6), and blue runs through the whole service — the lace on the golden plate (Exodus 28:37), the plate itself engraved HOLINESS TO THE LORD (Exodus 28:36).

The Urim and the Thummim

In the breastplate, over Aaron's heart: “And thou shalt put in the breastplate of judgment the Urim and the Thummim; and they shall be upon Aaron's heart, when he goeth in before the LORD” (Exodus 28:30). Moses put them in when Levi's son was dressed for the service (Leviticus 8:8). By them the priest asked counsel for Israel (Numbers 27:21); when Saul asked and heaven was silent, the silence itself is recorded — “he answered him not, neither by dreams, nor by Urim, nor by prophets” (1 Samuel 28:6); and a matter too hard waited “till there stood up a priest with Urim and with Thummim” (Ezra 2:63).

Their entries: Urim — H224 · Thummim — H8550

Tradition, labelled: Scripture never describes what the Urim and Thummim looked like, nor their colours. The familiar picture of a white stone and a black stone, drawn for yes and no, is tradition handed down outside Scripture — carried here as tradition, never as the text.

Signs and Answers · Every form with its chapter and verse

Signs, and the answers God gives

Signs and answers God gives in various forms — by the breastplate, by lot, by dream, by a sign asked, by the heavens, by angels and by prophets. And the caution side stands with them, the same honesty pattern as the census: a sign can come to pass and still be a test. The short line under each form is ours, and labelled so; the verses are Scripture, quoted from this device.

The caution, at the head of all of it — it stands before every entry below. Men “became vain in their imaginations” (Romans 1:21). “An evil and adulterous generation seeketh after a sign” (Matthew 12:39); “A wicked and adulterous generation seeketh after a sign” (Matthew 16:4). And a sign or a wonder that comes to pass can still be a test (Deuteronomy 13:1–3). Read every form below under this head: the sign is never the point — the God who gives it is.
